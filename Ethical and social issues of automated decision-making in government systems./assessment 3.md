## 1. Introduction

Automated Decision-Making (ADM) is the practice of employing computer systems, algorithms or artificial intelligence to help or make decisions that impact people. These systems can be used by governments to determine eligibility for services, to process applications and to allocate public resources. While ADM can help to reduce inefficiencies and inconsistencies, it can also present potential challenges related to bias, privacy, transparency, accountability, and fairness.

The role of ICT professionals is to take into account these risks as the government's decision could have a huge impact on people's rights and opportunities. The six policies, reports and frameworks, published or updated 2024 onwards, are explored in this portfolio. It highlights the Australian focus and the similarities and differences between Australia's and Canada's strategies on responsible automated decision-making.

## 2. Artefact One: Australian Policy for the Responsible Use of AI in Government (2025)

The Australian Government Digital Transformation Agency has published a policy for the responsible use of AI in government – Version 2.0.

This policy sets out expectations of Australian government agencies in their use of artificial intelligence. It addresses responsible AI governance and principles of accountability, transparency and the need for agencies to manage risks when introducing AI tools into their work (Digital Transformation Agency 2025).

This artefact was chosen because it is a technical artefact that shows that the introduction of AI into government is more than a technical choice. Systems need to be managed responsibly and their potential impacts need to be considered. Before any AI system can be put in place to make decisions that impact citizens, ICT professionals should ask themselves if the system is fair and suitable. This source explained to me the importance of being technically competent and ethical with your implementation.

## 3. Artefact Two: OAIC Report on Automated Decision-Making Transparency (2026)

The text is taken from the Office of the Australian Information Commissioner's publication "Automated decision-making and public reporting under the Freedom of Information Act".

The OAIC looked at how the Australian government agencies report on the use of automated decision making. The results of its research have revealed the lack of consistency in public disclosure, indicating that information on the use of ADM was not easily accessible from all of the agencies analysed (Office of the Australian Information Commissioner 2026).

I selected this report as it is one of the fundamental principles of transparency that enables individuals to be aware of the extent to which automated systems are likely to impact their decisions. People might have a hard time challenging a wrong or unjust result if the decision is based on an algorithm that wasn't disclosed by a government agency. Citizens should have the opportunity to be informed about the nature and process of making decisions and how to question them. This artefact deepened my knowledge of public trust and the fact that it is not just about the accuracy of software, but also openness and accountability.

## 4. Artefact Three: Australia's Automated Decision-Making Reform Consultation (2024–2025)

This is based on the consultation paper, Automated Decision-Making Reform, from the Australian Attorney-General's Department.

The Australian Attorney-General's Department has undertaken a consultation process with a view to reforming the laws in relation to automated government decisions. The consultation took place between November 2024 and January 2025 and included a discussion on the potential for a legal strategy to address issues raised by ADM (Attorney-General's Department 2025).

This artefact is relevant because it demonstrates that while automated decisions can be made at a team level, there are also legal and institutional considerations to take into account for the risks associated with automated decisions. Automated systems can impact availability of public services, financial support and other significant opportunities. I think there should be means to review and question decisions, to get a proper review. This source enabled me to be aware of the broader legal and social context in which ICTs are used.

## 5. Artefact Four: Canada's Directive on Automated Decision-Making (2025)

This policy was sourced from the Treasury Board of Canada Secretariat, Directive on Automated Decision-Making.

The Directive on Automated Decision-Making prescribes standards with the aim of enhancing the accountability, transparency and procedural fairness of automated decisions made by government institutions. It also sets out criteria for evaluating impacts and risks for covered systems (Treasury Board of Canada Secretariat 2025).

This artefact was chosen to make a comparison between the approach of Canada and Australia. It illustrates the approach that government organisations can take to setting formal requirements prior to deployment of automated systems. I believe this is relevant as that should not happen if an individual has been impacted by a government decision – there should be some sort of explanation or avenue for review. These countries were a good comparison for me to see that there was a need for institutional protection of responsible technology, and that there had to be people who could develop technology responsibly. Ethical responsibility needs to be built into the system design and governance, not added as an after-thought.

## 6. Artefact Five: Australia's National AI Assurance Framework (2024)

**Source:** Australian Government, National AI Assurance Framework.

The Australian Government published in 2024 a national AI assurance framework to help create a consistent approach to the responsible development of AI. Assurance frameworks can support organisations to identify the risks, think about what might be done to protect them, and show how the AI systems are being managed (Australian Government 2024).

This artefact is relevant because ethical risks need to be considered while developing and using a system. For instance, a government agency would need to take in account the quality of the information that they collect, the effects of wrong forecasts and who it might affect. I think it is better to get a grip on these issues at an early stage rather than when citizens are being harmed. This source made me realize that while the technical performance of a system is important, it's not the only factor to consider when assessing responsible AI.

## 7. Artefact Six: Canada's Algorithmic Impact Assessment (2024)

**Source:** Treasury Board of Canada Secretariat, Algorithmic Impact Assessment.

Canada's Algorithmic Impact Assessment (AIA) is a risk-assessment tool designed to identify the potential impact of automated decision systems. It considers factors such as system design, data, the type of decision and the people affected. Its assessment helps government departments determine the level of risk and identify suitable mitigation measures (Treasury Board of Canada Secretariat n.d.).

I selected this artefact because it provides a practical example of assessing risks before automated decisions affect people. It complements Canada's Directive on Automated Decision-Making by showing how risk assessment can support responsible implementation. I believe ICT professionals should consider possible effects on individuals and communities rather than focusing only on system performance. This source helped me understand that ethical responsibility involves identifying risks early, documenting them and reviewing systems when circumstances change.

## 8. Conclusion

While the processing of information provided by Automated Decision-Making may prove to be efficient for government, it is also likely to raise a number of serious ethical and social issues. Key themes emerging from the sources in this portfolio are transparency, fairness, accountability, risk assessment and appropriate safeguards. Examples of governmental initiatives designed to promote responsible use of automated systems are available in Australia and Canada.

I feel as an ICT professional, technology needs to be used for people, not to be efficient. Developers and organisations should think about who may be adversely affected, whether the decision can be explained and how those affected by it can look the decision over. This portfolio has enabled me to see the importance of working ethically in the design, development and assessment of government technology.
